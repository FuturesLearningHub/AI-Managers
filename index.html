Which tool, for which moment.

Vanderbilt gives you three approved tools, and managers lose more time choosing between them than using them. You do not need to compare features. One question sorts almost every case: how sensitive is the material you are about to paste in? Read the three cards, then try the drill underneath.

Three cases. Pick the tool.

Nothing is scored or sent anywhere. Pick an answer and the reasoning appears underneath.

Case 1. A Teams meeting just ended and you want a recap with owners and due dates. The transcript is sitting in Teams.

Copilot. The transcript already lives in your Microsoft 365 workspace, so nothing has to be pasted anywhere. Amplify would also be safe, and it is the right call if the note-taker is where your transcript actually lives. ChatGPT EDU is the wrong home for an internal meeting.

Case 2. You want to sharpen an opening paragraph for a talk about leadership. No Vanderbilt specifics, no names, nothing internal.

ChatGPT EDU. Nothing internal or personal is involved, so the strongest general-purpose model is a fair choice. Amplify would work too and costs you nothing to prefer. The point of this case is that the lower-level tool is genuinely fine when the material is generic.

Case 3. A team member disclosed a medical condition and you want help thinking through a schedule accommodation.

None of them. Health information about a person is protected health information, and no approved tool takes it. Take the accommodation to HR and your HR business partner. If you use AI at all here, it is to think about a generic scheduling question with no person attached, no condition named, and no detail that could identify anyone.

Module 01

Now the frame that goes around every one of these tools, in four words.

Module 01 of 5. About two minutes. Module 01 · Ethical and inclusive practice

Four anchors hold the frame.

AI drafts fast and it sounds certain, which is exactly the problem. Certainty is the model’s writing style, not evidence that it is right. The leader holds the frame, and four questions from Harvard Business Review’s AI leadership guidance hold it for you6. Run any output through these before you act on it. It takes about fifteen seconds once you know them.

Anchor 01
Authority

You own the final draft, not the tool. Edit it into your voice before it leaves you. If a message goes out sounding like nobody wrote it, your team will notice, and what they will conclude is that you did not think about them.

Ask: am I editing this in my voice?

Anchor 02
Purpose

Tie the output to a goal you can name, near-term or long-term. If it does not advance the goal, it is noise, and polished noise takes up more of your team’s attention than rough signal does.

Ask: which goal does this serve?

Anchor 03
Accountability

You bear the consequences. Own the call, not the model. No one on the receiving end of a bad decision has ever been comforted by hearing which tool helped make it.

Ask: if this fails, whose name is on it?

Anchor 04
Truth

Verify the facts, ask for alternatives, and test what could be wrong before you act. Check any number, quote, or policy the model hands you, especially the ones that sound most quotable.

Ask: what could this be wrong about, and how would I know?

Authority, Purpose, Accountability, Truth. Four questions, fifteen seconds, every time.

Try it · 45 seconds

Which anchor did they skip?

Three managers, three near misses. Name the anchor that would have caught it.

1. A manager sends the AI’s draft announcement to the whole team without changing a word. Two people reply asking whether a human wrote it.

Authority. The draft left the building in the model’s voice instead of the manager’s. One editing pass would have fixed it, and the team would have heard their manager instead of a template.

2. A manager quotes a policy detail from an AI summary in a memo to their director. The policy changed last year and the summary was describing the old version.

Truth. A confident sentence about policy is exactly the kind of claim to check before it appears in a memo with your name on it. Confidence is the model’s style, not its evidence.

3. A manager runs a weekly report through AI, sends it out every Friday, and cannot say what anyone does with it. It has gone out for four months.

Purpose. Faster production of something nobody reads is not a win, it is the same waste at higher speed. If it does not advance a goal you can name, the right move is to stop sending it.

Together

Forty-five seconds, out loud, in pairs. Each person picks one AI output they generated this week and answers all four questions to their partner. The partner’s only job is to notice which answer came hardest.

Do it now · 45 seconds

Pick any AI output you generated this week and answer these out loud: Am I editing this in my voice? Which goal does this serve? If it fails, whose name is on it? What could this be wrong about, and how would I know? The one that is hardest to answer is the one worth your next five minutes.

Module 02

One prompt pattern that handles almost everything a manager writes.

Module 02 of 5. About three minutes. Module 02 · Direction and delivery · Connection and advocacy

One pattern writes anything.

Almost everything a manager writes is one of three things: an announcement, an ask, or the opening of a hard conversation. You do not need three techniques for that. You need one pattern, five parts, and it works the same in all three tools.

The pattern

Give the model the same five things you would give a capable new hire before handing them a draft: who they are being, who is on the other end, what you want, what the limits are, and what shape it should come back in. Then add one line that changes everything: ask me one clarifying question before you draft.

That last line is the difference between a generic draft and a useful one. A model that asks first writes second, and the question it asks is often the thing you had not decided yet.

  • RoleWho the model is being while it helps you.
  • ContextWho receives this, what they already know, how they might feel.
  • TaskThe specific thing you want written, and what it has to achieve.
  • ConstraintsTone, length, vocabulary, and what it must not do.
  • FormatThe shape it comes back in: email with a subject line, bullets, two paragraphs.
The universal pattern 
Role: You are helping a manager at a large research university.
Context: [Who receives this, what they already know, how they might feel.]
Task: Draft a [message / agenda / feedback / talking points] that [specific goal].
Constraints: Warm but direct. Under [X] words. No jargon. Do not invent policy.
Format: [Email with subject line / bullets / two paragraphs and a closing question.]

Ask me one clarifying question before you draft.
Try it in
Amplify Recommended Manager messages usually carry internal context about your own team. Amplify is the right home, and the when-in-doubt choice. Copilot Also fine The better choice when you are replying inside an Outlook thread that is already open. ChatGPT EDU Only if generic Fine when the message names no person, no team, and nothing internal. Strip the specifics or use Amplify instead.
What comes back

A question first, then a draft that is close. It will be a little too smooth and a little too long, and it will use one word you would never say. That is the expected result, not a failure. The draft is the raw material; the next block is where it becomes yours.

The rewrite move
  • Paste the draft back with your voice in it. “Rewrite this in my voice. I tend to be direct, warm, and a little informal. Keep the structure.”
  • Two rounds is usually enough. If the third round is not better than the second, the remaining work is yours to do by hand.
  • Read the last line out loud before you send. Whatever your team remembers, it will be that one.

Module 03

The same discipline, aimed at the conversation you are about to have.

Module 03 of 5. About three minutes. Module 03 · Growth and recognition · Support and empowerment

Prepare the conversation you are about to have.

A 2026 scoping review of AI coaching research found that these tools are genuinely useful for the structured parts of coaching: goal setting, reflection prompts, and preparing feedback. The same review is clear about the limits. The model cannot read a room, and it misses cultural nuance that a human coach would catch7. So use it as your prep partner and keep the conversation yours.

The line that matters

There is a real difference between preparing a conversation and scripting one. Preparation gives you better questions and a clearer sense of what you are trying to do. A script gives you a performance, and the person across from you can tell.

Every prompt in this module ends with a version of the same instruction: do not write scripts. Keep it there. It is the difference between walking in ready and walking in reading.

Prompt · 1:1 preparation 
I have a 1:1 tomorrow with a direct report. Refer to them only by role, not by name.
Here are my rough notes from the last two weeks: [paste your notes].
Their current stretch goal is: [goal].

Give me:
1. Three open questions that invite reflection, not yes or no answers.
2. One thing worth recognizing specifically, tied to something in the notes.
3. One growth edge to explore, phrased as a question rather than a verdict.
4. A soft closing that hands the next step to them.

Do not write scripts. I want prompts I can adapt in the moment.
Try it in
Amplify Recommended Your notes describe a real person’s work. Keep them inside a Vanderbilt tool, with the person described by role only. Copilot Also fine Approved through your Vanderbilt account, and convenient if your notes already live in OneNote or Word. ChatGPT EDU Sits this one out Notes about a specific employee’s performance are internal. They stay in Amplify or Copilot.
Prompt · feedback drafting 
Help me draft feedback for a team member, described by role only.
Situation: [what happened, when, who was affected].
Behavior: [specifically what they did or did not do].
Impact: [on the team, the project, or our partners].

Give me two versions:
A) Reinforcing feedback, if the behavior was strong.
B) Redirecting feedback, if it needs to change.

Both under 90 words, second person, ending with an invitation to respond.
Do not use the word "just." Do not soften with "great job, but."

Situation, Behavior, Impact is a standard feedback structure. Naming all three keeps feedback about what happened rather than about who someone is.

Try it · 45 seconds

Two drafts came back. Which one do you send?

1. You asked for redirecting feedback about missed handoffs. Which draft goes out?

The second. It names the situation, the behavior, and the impact, and it ends by inviting a response instead of closing the topic. The first buries the message under praise, uses just to shrink it, and gives the person nothing specific to change.

2. The AI hands you a full script for the whole 1:1, opening line to closing line. What do you do with it?

Keep the questions, drop the script. The review that grounds this module is direct about it: the model cannot read the room7. Questions travel into a live conversation; a script only survives if the other person says their lines.

The five-minute pre-1:1 ritual: notes into Amplify, run the prep prompt, pick the two questions that feel most alive, bring paper, close the laptop, ask.

Together

In pairs, three minutes each way. One person describes a 1:1 they have coming up, by role only. The partner asks the three open questions from the prompt as if they were the manager. Then swap, and each person names the one question they want to keep.

Pause + reflect

Look at your next 1:1 on the calendar. Copy the prep prompt, paste your last two weeks of notes with the name removed, and run it before that meeting. Five minutes of prep changes what the half hour is worth.

Module 04

Same tools, aimed at the call everyone is waiting on.

Module 04 of 5. About three minutes. Module 04 · Change and collective learning

Wider options before the call.

AI is an extremely persuasive summarizer, and that is the risk. It can argue any side well, which means a strong case for the option you already liked tells you almost nothing. Use it to widen what you can see and to attack your own plan. Then apply judgment, because the judgment is the part that was always yours.

Two prompts, in this order

The first prompt widens: it asks for options that are genuinely different from each other, not three shades of the same idea, plus an early signal you could watch for to tell whether each one is working.

The second prompt attacks: it assumes your decision already failed and asks for the retrospective. This is called a premortem, and it works because it is far easier to explain a failure that has happened than to imagine one that has not.

Prompt 01 · options and tradeoffs 
I am deciding whether to [decision]. Here is what I know: [context].
The stakeholders are: [list them by role]. Constraints: [budget, timeline, policy].

Give me:
1. Three distinct options. Not variations on one idea.
2. For each: the strongest case, the biggest risk, and one signal
   I could look for in the next 30 days to know if it is working.
3. One option I probably have not considered.

End with the three questions I should be able to answer before I decide.
Prompt 02 · the premortem 
Assume the decision I just described failed badly, six months from now.
Write the memo my leadership would send explaining what went wrong.

Then work backward: what were the three earliest warning signs we ignored?
What would a version of me who saw them coming have done differently?

Run this one second, on the option you are leaning toward. Read it twice. It is the part managers skip, and it is the part that pays.

Try these in
Amplify Recommended Real decisions carry budget, staffing, and operational detail about your own unit. That is Levels 1 to 3, and it belongs here. Copilot Also fine Useful when the numbers behind the decision are already sitting in an Excel file. ChatGPT EDU Sits this one out Budget and staffing detail about your own operation is internal. Send it to Amplify.

The one thing AI cannot do for you: own the decision. Run the four anchors again before you commit, and lean hardest on Truth.

Try it · 30 seconds

The output is in. What is your next move?

1. You asked for three options and the model came back strongly recommending option A, with a confident case.

Ask for the counter-case. The model can argue any side persuasively, so a strong case for A tells you little until you have read the strongest case against it. Once you have both, the call is yours to make and yours to own.

2. The premortem names a failure you cannot rule out. What does that finding become?

It becomes a to-check item. A premortem finding is not a veto and it is not paperwork. Every failure reason you cannot rule out is either a question to answer now or a signal to put on your calendar for later.

Together

Take one real open decision from the room, described by role only. The instructor runs the options prompt live, then the premortem. Tables split the output: one takes the warning signs, one takes the option nobody considered, one takes the three questions. Each reports back in a sentence.

Pause + reflect

Name the decision that has been sitting on your desk longest. Then answer one question honestly: are you waiting because it is genuinely hard, or because you have not yet seen the option that makes it easy? The first prompt is for the second case.

The four rules that keep all of this safe.

The rules that make the rest of this safe to use. Guardrails / 09

The four rules.

Managers work with people, money, and records, so this course carries the strictest guardrails in the program. The first rule is the one that matters most, and it is the one people break by accident.

Rule 01
Data classification decides

Level 4, PHI, and HIPAA data go into none of these tools. Neither does information about children. Levels 1 to 3 belong in Amplify when you are unsure. Describe people by role, never by name. Anything that is really a personnel matter goes to HR and your HR business partner, not a chatbot.

Rule 02
The output is a draft, not a decision

The model can inform your thinking and it can propose options. It never makes the call. You design its role for each task, and the judgment stays with you because the consequences do.

Rule 03
Interrogate confident answers

Ask what is missing, ask for the counter-case, and check any number, quote, or policy before it moves anything. Confidence is the model’s writing style, not a measure of accuracy. Nothing in this course changes that.

Rule 04
Own it, in your voice

Edit every draft before it leaves you. Own the message and the call, and disclose substantive AI help to the people who rely on it. If you would be uncomfortable saying how a document was written, that discomfort is information.

Four quick questions to check that it stuck.

Four questions, and none of them are tricks. Knowledge check / 11

What you’ll remember.

Pick an answer on each and you will see right away how you did. Nothing here is scored or sent anywhere. If the version of this course in your learning system includes a graded check, that one replaces this.

1. You are about to paste something into an AI tool and you cannot remember how it is classified. What do you do?

Amplify, and stop if you suspect it is Level 4, PHI, or HIPAA. Deleting a chat afterwards does not undo the paste, and uncertainty about classification is itself the answer: it goes to the most protected place you have, or nowhere at all.

2. Two prompts for the same announcement. Which one gets the more useful draft?

The second. It carries all five parts of the pattern, and the clarifying line makes the model ask before it writes. The question it asks is usually the part of the announcement you had not decided yet, which is worth more than the draft.

3. A manager pastes a team member’s performance review into an AI tool to think through a role change. What went wrong?

Rule one, the strictest in the program. Approved tools are for operational detail, not personal records, and no prompt fix makes a performance review safe to paste. Take the matter to HR. If you use AI to think about the situation at all, describe it as a role with nothing that could identify anyone.

4. The AI hands you a polished draft that reads well and sounds nothing like you. Which anchor is about to be tested?

Authority. Polish is not the same as ownership, and a team can tell the difference between a message from their manager and a message from a template. One editing pass is the whole fix. Truth still needs its own pass on any number or policy in there.

Glossary

Every technical word this course used.

One line each, in the order you met them.

  • AI tool. Software you type an instruction into and get written work back. Amplify, Copilot, and ChatGPT EDU are the three Vanderbilt approves.
  • Prompt. The instruction you type. Everything in this course is about writing better ones.
  • Model. The system underneath the tool that produces the text. When this course says the model sounds confident, it means the writing style, not a claim about accuracy.
  • Data level. Vanderbilt’s label for how sensitive information is, from 1 (public) to 4 (most restricted).
  • PHI. Protected health information: anything about a person’s health that could identify them.
  • HIPAA. The federal law governing protected health information.
  • SSO. Single sign-on: logging in with your Vanderbilt account rather than a separate password.
  • Custom assistant, custom GPT. A saved setup that starts every chat with instructions you wrote once.
  • Transcript. The text record a meeting tool makes of what was said.
  • Premortem. Assuming a decision has already failed and writing the reasons, so the risks surface before you commit.
  • Second-order effect. What happens because of what happens: the consequence of the consequence.
  • Situation, Behavior, Impact. A feedback structure: what the moment was, what the person did, what followed.
  • Reinforcing and redirecting feedback. Feedback that asks for more of something, and feedback that asks for something different.
  • Stretch goal. A goal set slightly beyond current reach, used in development conversations.
  • Triage. Sorting a pile by what it needs rather than working through it in order.

Leading with AI

Vanderbilt University · The CHART Program · A Managers Voyage course · me5231979.github.io/AI-Managers

The frame: four anchors, fifteen seconds

Authority: you own the final draft. Edit it in your voice before it leaves you. Ask: am I editing this in my voice?

Purpose: tie the output to a goal you can name. If it does not advance the goal, it is noise. Ask: which goal does this serve?

Accountability: you bear the consequences. Own the call, not the model. Ask: if this fails, whose name is on it?

Truth: verify facts, seek alternatives, test what could be wrong. Ask: what could this be wrong about, and how would I know?

Data classification decides. Level 4, PHI, HIPAA, and information about children go into no tool at all.

The pattern that writes almost anything

Role: You are helping a manager at a large research university. Context: [Who receives this, what they already know, how they might feel.] Task: Draft a [message / agenda / feedback / talking points] that [specific goal]. Constraints: Warm but direct. Under [X] words. No jargon. Do not invent policy. Format: [Email with subject line / bullets / two paragraphs and a closing question.] Ask me one clarifying question before you draft.

The rewrite move: paste the draft back with “Rewrite this in my voice. I tend to be [three adjectives]. Keep the structure.” Two rounds is usually enough.

Prompts you can use this week

1 · 1:1 preparation. Amplify or Copilot; describe the person by role only

I have a 1:1 tomorrow with a direct report. Refer to them only by role, not by name. Here are my rough notes from the last two weeks: [paste your notes]. Their current stretch goal is: [goal]. Give me: 1. Three open questions that invite reflection, not yes or no answers. 2. One thing worth recognizing specifically, tied to something in the notes. 3. One growth edge to explore, phrased as a question rather than a verdict. 4. A soft closing that hands the next step to them. Do not write scripts. I want prompts I can adapt in the moment.

2 · Feedback drafting, using Situation, Behavior, Impact

Help me draft feedback for a team member, described by role only. Situation: [what happened, when, who was affected]. Behavior: [specifically what they did or did not do]. Impact: [on the team, the project, or our partners]. Give me two versions: A) Reinforcing feedback, if the behavior was strong. B) Redirecting feedback, if it needs to change. Both under 90 words, second person, ending with an invitation to respond. Do not use the word "just." Do not soften with "great job, but."

3 · Options and tradeoffs, before a decision

I am deciding whether to [decision]. Here is what I know: [context]. The stakeholders are: [list them by role]. Constraints: [budget, timeline, policy]. Give me: 1. Three distinct options. Not variations on one idea. 2. For each: the strongest case, the biggest risk, and one signal I could look for in the next 30 days to know if it is working. 3. One option I probably have not considered. End with the three questions I should be able to answer before I decide.

4 · The premortem, run second, on the option you are leaning toward

Assume the decision I just described failed badly, six months from now. Write the memo my leadership would send explaining what went wrong. Then work backward: what were the three earliest warning signs we ignored? What would a version of me who saw them coming have done differently?

The guardrails

  1. Data classification decides. Level 4, PHI, HIPAA, and information about children go into no tool at all. Levels 1 to 3 go to Amplify when you are unsure. People are described by ROLE, NEVER BY NAME. Personnel matters go to HR and your HR business partner.
  2. The output is a draft, not a decision. The model informs and proposes; you make the call.
  3. Interrogate confident answers. Ask what is missing, ask for the counter-case, check every number, quote, and policy.
  4. Own it, in your voice. Edit before it leaves you, and disclose substantive AI help to the people who rely on it.
