Leading with AI

The frame: four anchors, fifteen seconds

Authority: you own the final draft. Edit it in your voice before it leaves you. Ask: am I editing this in my voice?

Purpose: tie the output to a goal you can name. If it does not advance the goal, it is noise. Ask: which goal does this serve?

Accountability: you bear the consequences. Own the call, not the model. Ask: if this fails, whose name is on it?

Truth: verify facts, seek alternatives, test what could be wrong. Ask: what could this be wrong about, and how would I know?

Data classification decides. Level 4, PHI, HIPAA, and information about children go into no tool at all.

The pattern that writes almost anything

Role: You are helping a manager at a large research university. Context: [Who receives this, what they already know, how they might feel.] Task: Draft a [message / agenda / feedback / talking points] that [specific goal]. Constraints: Warm but direct. Under [X] words. No jargon. Do not invent policy. Format: [Email with subject line / bullets / two paragraphs and a closing question.] Ask me one clarifying question before you draft.

The rewrite move: paste the draft back with “Rewrite this in my voice. I tend to be [three adjectives]. Keep the structure.” Two rounds is usually enough.

Prompts you can use this week

1 · 1:1 preparation. Amplify or Copilot; describe the person by role only

I have a 1:1 tomorrow with a direct report. Refer to them only by role, not by name. Here are my rough notes from the last two weeks: [paste your notes]. Their current stretch goal is: [goal]. Give me: 1. Three open questions that invite reflection, not yes or no answers. 2. One thing worth recognizing specifically, tied to something in the notes. 3. One growth edge to explore, phrased as a question rather than a verdict. 4. A soft closing that hands the next step to them. Do not write scripts. I want prompts I can adapt in the moment.

2 · Feedback drafting, using Situation, Behavior, Impact

Help me draft feedback for a team member, described by role only. Situation: [what happened, when, who was affected]. Behavior: [specifically what they did or did not do]. Impact: [on the team, the project, or our partners]. Give me two versions: A) Reinforcing feedback, if the behavior was strong. B) Redirecting feedback, if it needs to change. Both under 90 words, second person, ending with an invitation to respond. Do not use the word "just." Do not soften with "great job, but."

3 · Options and tradeoffs, before a decision

I am deciding whether to [decision]. Here is what I know: [context]. The stakeholders are: [list them by role]. Constraints: [budget, timeline, policy]. Give me: 1. Three distinct options. Not variations on one idea. 2. For each: the strongest case, the biggest risk, and one signal I could look for in the next 30 days to know if it is working. 3. One option I probably have not considered. End with the three questions I should be able to answer before I decide.

4 · The premortem, run second, on the option you are leaning toward

Assume the decision I just described failed badly, six months from now. Write the memo my leadership would send explaining what went wrong. Then work backward: what were the three earliest warning signs we ignored? What would a version of me who saw them coming have done differently?

The guardrails

  1. Data classification decides. Level 4, PHI, HIPAA, and information about children go into no tool at all. Levels 1 to 3 go to Amplify when you are unsure. People are described by ROLE, NEVER BY NAME. Personnel matters go to HR and your HR business partner.
  2. The output is a draft, not a decision. The model informs and proposes; you make the call.
  3. Interrogate confident answers. Ask what is missing, ask for the counter-case, check every number, quote, and policy.
  4. Own it, in your voice. Edit before it leaves you, and disclose substantive AI help to the people who rely on it.
